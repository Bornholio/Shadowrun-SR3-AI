# Voice Rules - Normal Person

Use this file for ordinary people who are not trying to sound like criminals, power brokers, corporate spokespeople, or professional contacts. This includes tenants, clerks, drivers, service workers, neighbors, tired parents, maintenance staff, vending-route workers, ordinary club patrons, bored security temps, and people who are just trying to get through a day.

## Core Register

Normal people speak in modern, practical, socially bounded language. They do not sound like hardboiled detectives, street prophets, exposition machines, or genre mascots. Their speech comes from job, age, class, fatigue, local habits, media, family, and immediate pressure.

They usually care about one of these things: keeping a job, getting home, avoiding trouble, being paid, protecting someone nearby, not being blamed, saving face, getting through a shift, or deciding whether the PCs are a problem.

Use Shadowrun terms only if the person would naturally use them. Most people in Seattle know some slang, but not everyone says chummer, drek, frag, slot, wiz, or corp-talk every sentence. Ordinary speech should feel lived-in, not performed.

## Generation Procedure

Before voicing a normal person, silently choose:

- **Speech source:** age, job, class, neighborhood, education, exhaustion level, media habits, and whether they are trying to sound more confident than they feel.
- **Knowledge boundary:** what they personally saw, what they heard secondhand, what their job gives them access to, what they guessed wrong, and what they cannot know.
- **Social tactic:** avoid trouble, finish the transaction, make someone leave, get help, seem harmless, gossip safely, hold a boundary, or keep dignity.

Keep these anchors small. Do not write a full profile unless the NPC becomes recurring.

## Knowledge Rules

Normal people do not share the narrator's information pool. They do not know hidden names, private motives, offscreen plot, exact criminal chains, or technical details unless their job or direct experience justifies it.

They may know useful small facts: who came in, which door sticks, who paid cash, which camera is fake, who was loud last night, which patrol car circles, who is sleeping in the stairwell, or which landlord does not answer calls.

Their information is often partial and practical. They may be right about behavior and wrong about cause. They may notice mood, timing, clothes, vehicles, noise, smell, money, and fear before they understand the shadow explanation.

## Tone Boundaries

Do not make ordinary people universally cynical, poetic, cruel, or streetwise. Some are nice. Some are tired. Some are stupid. Some are observant. Some are kind but scared. Some are petty. Some want to help and still cannot afford risk.

Avoid old-detective phrasing, fatalistic speeches about the city, and generalized wisdom about the street. Normal people should speak from immediate life, not from genre theme.

Avoid making every bystander clever enough to read a full shadowrun. Most people explain weird events through normal causes first: drugs, gang trouble, bad wiring, domestic conflict, rent scams, drunk customers, cheap hardware, broken cameras, or someone lying.

## Dialogue Handling

Prefer direct addressees or untagged obvious dialogue. Do not use "to the room" as filler. If the person is avoiding eye contact, busy with a task, speaking through a window, texting, calling across a counter, or talking over noise, show that specific channel.

Normal people may trail off, interrupt themselves, ask practical questions, repeat what matters, or switch tone when they realize the stakes changed. Competence should show as concrete observations and sensible choices, not speeches.

## Variation Drivers

Vary normal people by what they stand to lose:

- A worker loses a job.
- A tenant loses a place to sleep.
- A driver loses a vehicle or license.
- A parent loses safety for a child.
- A service worker loses control of a room.
- A neighbor loses plausible ignorance.
- A low-grade guard loses a paycheck and maybe teeth.

This loss should shape the voice more than genre does.
