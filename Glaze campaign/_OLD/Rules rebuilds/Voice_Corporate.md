# Voice Rules - Corporate

Use this file for corporate employees, managers, wageslaves, receptionists, security supervisors, legal staff, clinic administrators, executives, consultants, contractors, and anyone whose speech is shaped by hierarchy, liability, policy, and institutional survival.

## Core Register

Corporate voice is controlled, status-aware, and risk-managed. It is not automatically robotic, villainous, sterile, or monologue-heavy. Most corporate people are employees trying to survive inside a machine, not masterminds.

They may speak with polish, training, policy language, customer-service patience, managerial compression, technical focus, or executive threat disguised as courtesy. Their voice should reflect position in the ladder.

Corporate people often avoid direct admissions. They prefer process, authorization, documentation, scope, liability, schedule, compliance, service tiers, and who is allowed to know what.

## Generation Procedure

Before voicing a corporate NPC, silently choose:

- **Speech source:** rank, department, education, public-facing training, security role, technical specialty, class background, and how much power they actually have.
- **Knowledge boundary:** what their badge, records, job function, clearance, gossip, and personal observations allow them to know.
- **Social tactic:** control access, protect liability, collect information, redirect blame, preserve face, enforce policy, extract consent, stall, sell, intimidate politely, or make someone else own the risk.

Do not make all corporate characters share executive knowledge. Compartmentalization is part of the setting.

## Knowledge Rules

Corporate NPCs know their lane. A receptionist may know schedules and visitors but not black projects. A guard may know patrol routines and incident codes but not legal strategy. A manager may know budget pressure and staffing problems but not secret research. A clinic worker may know intake procedures and rumors but not who paid for a covert install.

Corporate records may be accurate, incomplete, sanitized, falsified, or inaccessible to the person speaking. The person may trust the record even when it is wrong.

Corporate NPCs should not casually know street names, buried identities, hidden runner capabilities, or private histories unless records, surveillance, informants, or prior scenes justify it.

## Tone Boundaries

Do not make every corporate speaker smug. Some are bored, scared, underpaid, ambitious, decent, burned out, officious, careful, or trapped by policy. Some want to help but only inside a form. Some enjoy power. Some hate the system but still enforce it.

Avoid speeches about capitalism, the Sixth World, or corporate evil unless the speaker has reason to make such a speech. Most corporate harm is procedural: denial, delay, security escalation, legal framing, data retention, pricing, credential checks, and quiet compartmentalization.

Avoid excessive futuristic jargon. Use concrete institutional language: file, badge, authorization, claim, account, shift, incident, escalation, compliance, contract, service level, liability, inventory, appointment, and supervisor.

## Dialogue Handling

Corporate speech often hides force inside politeness. It may ask questions that are really demands, offer options that are not choices, or apologize without yielding. It may become sharper when status is threatened or when the speaker realizes the PCs are outside normal process.

Do not use "talking to the room" as filler. Corporate speech is usually addressed through role: to the visitor, to security, to a subordinate, to a supervisor, into an intercom, into a call, or through a form.

## Variation Drivers

Use institutional stakes to differentiate voice:

- Reception loses control of access.
- Security loses face and safety.
- Management loses budget, records, and blame cover.
- Legal loses clean liability.
- Technical staff lose system integrity.
- Medical staff lose license, supplies, and patient control.
- Executives lose leverage, image, and deniability.
- Contractors lose the next contract.

Let rank and risk determine how much warmth, threat, detail, and honesty appear.
