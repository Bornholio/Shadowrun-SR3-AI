# SR3 Physical Area Security Ratings (AAA–Z)

Source: *New Seattle* (FASA, 1999), "Dealing With The Law," pp. 108–111; Burglary rules at p. 136. Neighborhood descriptions and district examples from the AREA lifestyle category in *Sprawl Survival Guide*. Scale originates in *Seattle Sourcebook* (FASA, 1990).

## What a Rating Represents

A security rating is defined by the amount of coverage Lone Star or any other security agency is paid to give an area. The ratings are universal and have entered common parlance — Renraku advertised their Seattle arcology as triple-A security for 100,000 people.

Lone Star's coverage ends where a corporation's property begins. A business in a Z-Zone may have Renraku Red Samurai or Knight Errant elite forces guarding it even though Lone Star will not enter the area for any amount of money.

The ratings track lifestyle tiers, and those tiers are not evenly spaced. AAA and AA are Luxury neighborhoods, A is High, B is Middle. C is Low and D is Squatter — a tenfold gap in what residents live on, hidden behind a single letter. E and Z are Street.

## The Ratings

### AAA

The highest available level of security, usually reserved for upper-class enclaves and businesses willing to pay to have the area outside their property patrolled. This is the only tier that buys proactive and deterrent measures rather than reaction. Visible and hidden security both run at once: PanicButton booths open and working, patrols on foot and in vehicles, astral patrols using spirits and magicians, security drones, and security riggers watching the cameras.

The police guarding AAA areas are the finest, with the most duty hours logged. Assignment here is the ultimate promotion and no one wants demotion for lack of effort. Response to even the most minor crime is practically immediate, and security providers are reluctant to leave any investigation open regardless of the cost of pursuing it. If closing an investigation means bringing in chumps to take the fall and releasing them later, that is what they do, because crime is not allowed to succeed in these areas.

This is one of the most prestigious parts of town, with private security patrolling in addition to the contracted city police. The neighborhood resembles a well-maintained park, kept that way by a small army of gardeners, janitors and handymen. *Examples: waterfront areas of Seattle.*

### AA

Usually reserved for High-lifestyle areas and businesses that want constant coverage but will not pay for around-the-clock deterrent measures. Patrols cover the area at all hours but less frequently. Astral patrols run on an irregular schedule, there may be no security rigger system set up at all, and drones and spirits stand in for humans in some places.

Response is prompt but takes a back seat to anything going down in a AAA area. Standard officers respond first and call in a specialist only if needed. Assignment here is also viewed as a promotion, so officers investigate complaints thoroughly, if not as quickly as one tier up.

The neighborhood is among the best you are likely to find, with good housing, places to go and things to do. Security presence is solid and anyone who looks like they don't belong is likely to be stopped. You are unlikely to become a victim of crime here. *Examples: parts of Bellevue, Downtown, Tacoma.*

### A

Most of the metroplex. This level covers any area with a Middle lifestyle, which accounts for the largest share of Seattle's residential sections. Security is excellent, but coverage decreases dramatically from AA, primarily because Lone Star lacks sufficient personnel. There are regular street patrols but only rare astral, drone or security-rigger presence.

Law enforcement here is essentially a call-and-response system, and it works because the residents take part. There are many PanicButton booths, neighborhood watch groups and other organizations willing to call in at the slightest hint of criminal activity. This community policing produces a higher number of false alarms, which in turn makes response time slower.

The neighborhood is a corporate enclave or a better residential area, with fairly nice houses or relatively well-run apartment buildings. The cops make an effort to respond to calls, and there is often a neighborhood watch keeping crime down. *Examples: Council Island, parts of Auburn, Bellevue, Downtown, Everett.*

### B

Usual for areas made up primarily of commercial rather than residential property — industrial parks, office complexes and suburban manufacturing, things that have existed side by side with middle-class residential areas since the 1960s. Lone Star classifies these as high criminal target areas, which is to say prime targets for shadowrunners, and responds accordingly.

Urban renewal is currently washing over these areas. Rehabbers are turning older buildings into art galleries, loft apartments and other low-middle-class businesses and dwellings, spreading Lone Star ever thinner and giving these areas a precarious hold on their B rating.

The neighborhood is a business district in one of the better parts of town. There is some crime, as everywhere, but you can walk the streets at night without body armor and a pistol. Not often, anyway. *Examples: much of Auburn, Fort Lewis, Renton.*

### C

The Low-lifestyle residential tier. Patrols are infrequent and PanicButton booths poorly maintained, so crime is reported less often and therefore enforced less often. The security services make no real effort to prevent crimes before they happen.

Lone Star receives a hostile reception from the general populace, which accuses law enforcement of being less than thorough in investigations. Most officers begin their training in areas like this and try to get promoted out as quickly as possible, though the savvy ones — especially detectives — maintain their contacts from their beat days, because this is where the information flows.

The neighborhood is a residential area that has seen better times: dilapidated buildings, poor maintenance and petty crime as part of daily life. The cops do sometimes show their faces, at least when something fairly bad happens. A typical part of the sprawl. *Examples: much of Everett, Snohomish, Tacoma.*

### D

The Squatter-lifestyle business tier — the same neglect as C, applied to commercial ground where almost nobody lives. Infrequent patrols, poorly maintained PanicButton booths, no attempt at prevention, and the same hostility toward the Star, sharpened by the accusation that they barely bother investigating when local businesses get hit.

The neighborhood is a dirty, run-down business district rather than a residential one. Few people live here because of that, and those who do are typically low-lifes of all sorts. Crime is common, and guarding yourself and your possessions is entirely your own problem. *Examples: parts of Auburn and Everett.*

### E

Slums. The institutional position is that the residents are not real people, because they have no SINs, so why make the effort. Lone Star assigns no patrols to such areas and makes no attempt to prevent crimes from happening.

They will respond if the victim has a SIN and calls in a complaint personally, if the violence makes the headlines, or if it threatens to spill over into more important areas. When the Star does respond, it does so at a very high level. The slums are an excellent place for detectives and undercover operatives to maintain contacts.

The area is filled with transients, refugees, street gangs, criminals and others living in abject poverty. Police presence is spotty at best and they do not involve themselves in what goes on unless they really have to, so for security the residents look to the local gang. *Examples: most of Redmond and Puyallup.*

### Z

The Z-Zones, where humanity has devolved to a state of tribes, anarchy and a complete lack of any kind of normal society. It is survival of the fittest, and the law does not care who wins and who loses as long as everyone stays within the boundaries. Containment is the whole of the policy: keeping the area sealed matters more than preventing or solving anything inside it. In many cases walls or natural boundaries physically separate these areas from the rest of the sprawl.

Only in pursuit of an investigation will the Star enter a Z-Zone, and when they do they come prepared for war. Lone Star considers Z-Zone duty to be hazard duty, and officers assigned to these details receive three times their normal wages and a week off after any operation.

This is the worst area possible, inhabited by the most desperate in society — the homeless, squatters, chipheads, ghouls and other cast-aways, metahuman or otherwise. Law enforcement never comes. Violence and crime are endemic and security is entirely in your own hands. *Examples: the deepest, darkest parts of Redmond and Puyallup.*

### Response Force Composition

- **AAA, AA, A** — initial patrol is one vehicle carrying two officers, with additional patrols arriving every second Initiative Pass until called off by headquarters.
- **B** — minimum four patrol officers with an elite officer and a security/combat mage on standby; initial response is two patrol cars.
- **C, D** — except for undercover officers and detectives, six to eight fully armored officers in an armored Citymaster with two patrol vehicles and one combat mage. Drones are sometimes used to reduce the risk of injury to officers.
- **E** — two Citymasters filled with twelve to fifteen officers in heavy armor, one rigger per vehicle to handle drones and the Citymaster's weapons, and at least two combat mages, always led by an elite officer.
- **Z** — minimum three Citymasters (twenty-five officers in full armor) with air support from armored helicopters; one rigger per vehicle handling weapon mounts and one extra maintaining drone coverage; at least five combat mages and one combat decker maintaining communication with headquarters; three elite officers running the show, one coordinating the mages, one in the air, one with the ground troops.

### Response Timing and the Combat Turn

A Combat Turn is 3 seconds. Even if someone called 911 before combat began, security cannot arrive in time to intervene in an ongoing firefight regardless of the security rating. A hundred Combat Turns — many days of play — is five minutes of game time.

The Security Template exists so the gamemaster can customize the response for each rating and send the law in to break up a firefight. The timing it represents is acknowledged as unrealistic. The table applies to Lone Star and other law enforcement only; once runners are on corporate property they are entirely in the corp's jurisdiction. Runners subject to ongoing investigations are handled as the gamemaster chooses.

### Template Notes

**Response Time is cumulative.** Each effect occurs a number of Initiative Passes after the last effect. Example: rolling 6 for Aware of Problem in a AA zone means Lone Star becomes aware on the second Initiative Pass (6÷3 = 2). Rolling 4 for First Response sends a scouting patrol on the third pass (4÷3 = 1).

Roll the sequence when a fight begins, note the passes on which each response lands, and adjust results as needed.

**Aware of Problem** — some event triggers awareness that a problem exists:

- **AAA, AA, A** — the first fired shot that can be heard, an explosion, a call into the law, or activation of a PanicButton
- **B, C** — a cannon fired, a weapon fired in auto or full-auto mode, or a rocket, missile or other materiel exploding, in addition to a call into the station or a PanicButton alert
- **D** — a rocket, missile, cannon or explosion in addition to a PanicButton alert or phone call
- **E** — repeated cannon, missile or rocket fire. A resident calling in a disturbance must provide a valid SIN.
- **Z** — the law never notices a problem.

**First Response** — always an astral mage or spirit and a security rigger on hidden camera. The security rigger is available only in AAA and AA areas, and the gamemaster may decide AA security provides one half the time.

**Second Response** — always a spotter drone. The enforcement company also alerts a patrol in the area at this stage.

**Patrol Arrives** — the initial metahuman response, using the compositions listed above.

**Reinforcements 1** — a Citymaster with six fully armored officers, one rigger for weapons and one combat mage.

**Reinforcements 2** — air support using VTOLs and drones, both armed and combat-ready.

**Continuing Response** — how often additional reinforcements arrive, of the same make-up as Reinforcements 1 or 2 at the gamemaster's discretion.

## House Rules: Response Timing

Everything below is a house rule, not RAW. The published Security Template above is unchanged.

### The Unit Problem

A Combat Turn is always 3 seconds. Initiative Passes subdivide those 3 seconds, and how many exist is set by whoever rolled highest initiative. One pass is therefore worth 0.75 seconds in a fight containing a wired samurai at 4 passes, and 3 seconds in an all-magician fight at 1 pass.

Run AAA Reinforcements 1 (D6, average 3.5) through both: the fast team gets an armored Citymaster with six officers in 2.6 seconds, the slow team in 10.5 seconds. Same neighborhood, same crime, four-fold difference — set entirely by the runners' reflexes, which Lone Star cannot know about. Adding a wired NPC to the fight accelerates the police response as well.

Initiative Passes are a unit of turn order, not of time. Any response clock has to be measured in something else.

### Real-World Anchors

Response decomposes into call processing, dispatch, and travel. NYC priority-1 figures run roughly 2.25 minutes call-taker processing, 1.46 minutes dispatch, and 4.03 minutes travel, totalling about 7.7 minutes. PERF put the US priority-1 average at 10 minutes 15 seconds in 2021. Urban emergency response generally falls between 5 and 10 minutes; rural routinely exceeds 15. Seattle critical response averaged 7 minutes 55 seconds in 2023. Degraded departments are the model for the low tiers — one city's high-priority average went from 12.7 to 19.1 minutes between 2018 and 2022.

Detection dominates the total. Recognition and reporting adds 4 to 11 minutes before the clock starts, so real elapsed time from first aggressive action to first officer on scene reaches 10 to 20 minutes. Consequently the fight is normally over first: 69% of active shooter incidents end within 5 minutes, and 75% of 2024 incidents ended before law enforcement arrived. Tactical teams are slower again — roughly 20 minutes for a fast SWAT team, 75 minutes to mobilise and enter at Las Vegas.

Sensor networks compress detection and dispatch only. ShotSpotter delivers a human-verified alert in under 60 seconds and claims time-to-dispatch drops from about 4.5 minutes to under 60 seconds, but the independent Durham pilot measured the real end-to-end gain at 1.2 minutes off median response. No sensor shortens travel.

### Why Sub-Minute Arrival Is Impossible

An emergency vehicle in dense urban traffic makes roughly 40 km/h effective, and street routing adds about 30% over straight-line distance. A 60-second arrival therefore requires the unit to have already been within about 500 m — one continuously available unit per square kilometre.

Staffing that: 168 hours of coverage divided by roughly 34 productive patrol hours per officer-week is about 5 officers per position; a two-officer car is about 10; adding supervision, dispatch and support gives roughly 15 sworn officers per km² for one always-staffed car.

In a small AAA enclave that works out to 125–200 sworn per 100,000 residents, below the US big-city norm of 200–400, and it is affordable only because near-zero call volume keeps units genuinely free. Real cities field 300+ per 100,000 and still average 7–8 minutes because units are tied up on other calls 60–75% of the time. Applying the same standard to A-rated ground — most of the metroplex, with real crime — requires roughly 3x nominal strength to keep one unit free, or about 1,300 sworn per 100,000. That is triple the densest real force in the United States. Sub-minute response does not scale beyond small enclaves.

The design conclusion: awareness and intervention must run on separate clocks. Sensors and drones scale with capital, bodies scale with payroll.

**Hard floor: nothing metahuman arrives in under 30 Combat Turns, anywhere, at any rating.** That is travel physics and no contract buys past it. The fastest cell on the table, AAA first patrol, bottoms out at 45 turns.

Calibration checks against the real figures above: A first patrol averages 9.5 minutes against Seattle's 7:55. C averages 18.5 against the degraded-city 19.1. A heavy response averages 32 minutes, inside the real SWAT band of 20 to 75. E runs past an hour, matching both the fiction and the worst real municipal collapses.

### Notes on Use

The drone and astral row is the one place the setting legitimately beats reality. A rigger jumped into a rooftop-cached rotodrone launches instantly, flies straight-line above traffic at 100+ km/h, and covers a kilometre in about 35 seconds. A spirit or projecting magician moves comparably fast astrally. This is a genuinely different vehicle, not a hand-wave, and it is why RAW First Response is specifically an astral mage or spirit plus a rigger on camera — the one part of the original template that survives contact with real numbers.

At A rating, first patrol averages 190 Combat Turns while a firefight runs 5 to 20. The law arrives well after the shooting stops, matching the real finding that most incidents end before police reach the scene. The table therefore functions as a getaway clock governing extraction, not as an in-combat timer.

In a AAA zone something is watching within 5 to 45 turns, but nothing can touch the runners before turn 45, and the Citymaster is 15 to 40 minutes out. The correct counters become drone jamming and astral masking rather than moving faster.

For set pieces that need the law to arrive mid-firefight, read every minute value on the table as Combat Turns instead. That preserves the relationships between tiers and keeps all numbers on the same 5-step grid.
