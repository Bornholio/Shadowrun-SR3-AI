# Malibu Tower Luxury Suite — Floors 22–23 Expanded

GM-facing location continuity for the duplex luxury residence occupied under the alias **Juan Herrero**. Juan Herrero is not a verified legal identity; the name functions as the Spanish equivalent of **John Smith**.

The suite has **three bedrooms** in practical use: one master suite and two secondary guest suites. It was configured for a single wealthy occupant who entertained selectively rather than for a large family. Several rooms were larger and more specialized than additional bedrooms would have been.

## General Character

The apartment was designed to appear restrained at first glance and overwhelming only after moving through it.

Materials were expensive but quiet: dark hardwood, polished stone, treated acoustic glass, hand-finished metal, sculpted wall panels, wool carpeting, layered lighting, and custom cabinetry with no exposed hardware. Very little looked mass-produced.

The residence was not cluttered. Its wealth appeared through scale, duplication, hidden systems, and the expectation that service happened automatically. Lights rose before an occupant entered. Glass turned opaque before privacy was requested. Climate followed room use. Coffee prepared itself. Elevator cars arrived based on departure patterns. Cleaning machines worked around occupied rooms without instruction.

The suite's strongest impression was that the building itself behaved like trained staff.

## Building Integration

The apartment was tied into Malibu Tower's private elevator network, garage access, building climate, secure telecom, service scheduling, and closed-circuit simsense security.

Its high-roller security could be isolated from normal tower monitoring. During isolation, tower staff could see that the suite was active and protected but could not casually read its internal alerts. A manual override was required to enter the security envelope or reconnect the suite to normal building services.

Wards enclosed several major residential zones, especially the private rooms, office, and principal access routes. They were not arranged as a single perfect shell. Three narrow astral approaches remained incompletely covered.

## Post-Operation Condition

By the end of Singer's clearance, the suite no longer resembled an occupied luxury residence.

Removed property included:

- the occupant's body;
- clothing and personal effects;
- portable electronics;
- headware data;
- documents and negotiation files;
- bar contents;
- robots and their charging hardware;
- major furniture;
- business telecom;
- the concealed safe;
- billiards table;
- built-in and base-mounted electronics that could be removed without destroying the tower;
- household supplies and valuable fittings;
- luxury objects and service equipment.

Rooms were bleached and treated for hair and skin residue. A small salamander singed remaining biological trace while a hearth spirit prevented fire or structural damage.

The billiards table's former position was deliberately scratched to imply professional movers.

The residence's morning routine continued during the clearance, creating false signs of occupancy.

Near the end, a silent alarm began flashing. The exact trigger was not identified. Shortly afterward, tower security registered a manual override condition involving services on the twenty-second floor.

The suite should appear to investigators as if a highly organized removal team emptied it in a compressed window, though the absence of ordinary elevator, freight, and moving traffic creates an impossible contradiction.

## GM Use

Use the suite as a high-technology luxury environment with magical security layered into architecture and infrastructure.

Its defining features are:

- scale without clutter;
- hidden systems instead of visible servants;
- robots performing routine labor;
- integrated security and telecom;
- smart rooms that anticipate occupancy;
- quiet wealth rather than gaudy display;
- wards and sensory systems that are strong but not perfect;
- enough physical property that clearing the suite feels industrial rather than domestic.

Do not treat every room as a separate plot. Most rooms are evidence of lifestyle, resources, habits, and capability. Only use room details when they affect movement, concealment, security, evidence, resources, or later recovery of removed property.
