# Scumbag — Basement Room Detail

The basement is a converted service level under the Bitter Lake/Bitter Hills property, not a buried dungeon. It was originally utility and storage space for a cheap residential-service building, then expanded by cutting rooms into the concrete and partitioning with washable wall panels, locked interior doors, cheap medical flooring, and exposed overhead conduits. A person walking it understands the place is illegal and controlled, but also practical: bright task lighting, floor drains, soap smell, bleach smell, covered bins, locked cabinets, plastic sheeting used as work protection, and enough organization to move people, parts, gear, and waste without leaving the whole building unusable afterward.

The main access is the reinforced door behind the ground-floor bar, disguised on the public side as a supply closet. That stair is narrow enough to slow furniture but wide enough for two people carrying a cooler, a folded stretcher, a tied carpet roll, or a sealed equipment case. The second path is the rear service route: from the basement service room up to the rear utility door and out into the north alley, where trash pickup, linen pickup, vending-route deliveries, small medical-supply boxes, and contractor visits can blend into ordinary service traffic. There are no dank sewers, no long hidden tunnels, and no magic escape route; getting things in and out means stairs, dollies, sealed bins, coolers, laundry bags, contractor crates, and a vehicle that can sit in the alley without looking impossible.

## Basement Stair / Inner Landing

The stair comes down from behind the bar into a short locked landing with hard light, scuffed walls, and a floor mat that catches water and grit from shoes. The ceiling is low enough to make tall people notice it, and the walls show damage from boxes, coolers, and equipment being dragged around corners. The door at the top is the real choke point; the landing itself is plain and functional, with no decoration and no reason for a customer to be here.

The landing splits the basement into work zones. One side leads toward storage, laundry, and service handling; the other side leads toward the clinic and chip-production rooms. A person standing here can hear mechanical hum, water through pipes, muffled movement above, and the sharper sound of metal shelving or cabinet doors if someone is working below.

Contents:

- Reinforced interior door — heavy supply-closet door at top of stairs with a serious mechanical lock and interior bar hardware.
- Concrete stair — narrow service stair with worn anti-slip strips and scraped sidewalls.
- Wall keypad — cheap but real lock control for the basement-side door.
- Floor mat and drain pan — catches wet shoes, mop water, and small spills.
- Utility light strips — bright, exposed, easy to replace.
- Wall hooks — used for aprons, jackets, gloves, and clipboards.
- Small camera — fixed view of the stairs and landing, wired into local recording gear.

## Service Storage / Intake Room

This room looks like the part of the basement a landlord or delivery worker could be told about if absolutely necessary. It has shelves, boxed supplies, cleaning goods, contractor junk, folded tarps, spare towels, cheap packaged linens, and several sealed plastic bins with labels that are boring enough not to invite reading. The room smells strongest of detergent and bleach, but under that is ordinary basement concrete, dust, and warm electrical equipment.

Most movement in and out passes through here because it gives everything a normal shape. A person carrying in gear can call it cleaning stock, bar stock, linen replacement, appliance repair, or maintenance. A person carrying things out can make it look like laundry, spoiled food, damaged furniture, medical waste pickup, or trash overflow. The room is not clean-room sterile; it is a working buffer between the public house and the illegal medical rooms.

Contents:

- Metal shelving — cleaning stock, spare linens, gloves, paper rolls, plastic wrap, and sealed supply boxes.
- Utility dolly — folding hand truck for bins, coolers, and equipment cases.
- Laundry hampers — heavy canvas and plastic bins sized for linens or bulky bundled loads.
- Contractor crates — hard-sided storage boxes with generic maintenance labels.
- Bleach and detergent stock — enough to support frequent cleaning without seeming unusual for a dirty rental business.
- Mop sink — stained utility sink with hose sprayer and wall-mounted chemical rack.
- Locked supply cabinet — basic tools, spare locks, tape, cutters, respirator masks, and packaged gloves.
- Rear service door controls — interior latch and alarm bypass tied to the rear utility exit.

## Rear Utility Exit / Alley Handling

The rear utility exit is the basement’s practical loading route. It does not open into a secret tunnel; it opens through a short service vestibule, up a half-flight or ramped concrete run, and out to the north alley behind the property. The outside door is plain, reinforced, and ugly, matching the kind of back-of-house access a cheap mixed-use rental might use for trash, maintenance, or deliveries. It can take a dolly, but not quietly if overloaded.

A person using this route deals with the real world: alley sightlines, neighbors’ rear windows, dogs, rain, trash bins, delivery schedules, and whether a van can stop without blocking someone. The route is useful because it is boring, not hidden. Coolers, laundry bags, contractor cases, and taped waste bins can move through here if timed around pickup, bar noise, or routine service traffic.

Contents:

- Reinforced exterior door — metal rear utility door with deadbolts and interior latch hardware.
- Short concrete ramp/run — rough service path from basement level up to the alley exit.
- Exterior light — bright utility lamp over the rear door.
- Trash bins — normal commercial/residential bins staged near the alley.
- Two lidded waste carts — washable carts that can pass as linen or bar waste handling.
- Hose bib — exterior water hookup near the door.
- Alley camera — cheap fixed camera covering the immediate rear door and bins.
- Rubber wheel chocks — used when a small van or handcart is held briefly at the rear.

## BTL Production Bay

The production bay is a cramped technical room with more electronics than comfort. The walls are covered with cheap acoustic foam, pinned cable runs, hardwired feeds, and mismatched work surfaces. The equipment is not sleek studio hardware; it is salvage, street repair, hobbyist parts, and low-grade professional kit forced to do steady dirty work. There are chairs, terminals, chip trays, external storage, and enough fans to make the room warm and buzzy.

A person walking through knows this room handles recording media, editing, burning, and storage. It looks like a dirty little production shop rather than a hacker temple. The cables are labeled in practical shorthand, the floor has taped cable paths, and most surfaces have bins for blanks, bad burns, adapters, and wiped cases. The room can be locked separately from the clinic and storage rooms.

Contents:

- Three rebuilt terminals — Rating 3-class cobbled workstations with mismatched cases and patched peripherals.
- Chipburner — Rating 2 jury-rigged optical/BTL burning setup on a reinforced table.
- Chip blank trays — sorted blank chips, cases, sleeves, and damaged rejects.
- External drives — portable and brick-style storage units kept in a locked drawer or cabinet.
- Cable patch board — hardwired room feeds and internal routing labels.
- Rolling work chair — cracked synthleather chair with worn arms.
- Two stools — cheap metal stools for short work or an assistant.
- Small parts bins — adapters, connectors, optical leads, labels, tape, and screws.
- Vent fan — noisy fan pulling heat out of the equipment corner.
- Locking file cabinet — physical notes, labels, receipts, spare cases, and removable storage.

## Medical Workroom

The medical workroom is low-grade but controlled. It is not a charnel house, and it is not a hospital. It looks like a street clinic built by people who care about usable surfaces, locks, water, light, refrigeration, and cleanup more than patient comfort. The walls are washable panels, the floor is sealed, the main table is battered but stable, and everything important is either locked, bagged, covered, or on wheeled trays. The smell is antiseptic, soap, bleach, plastic, and cold metal.

This room is where cyberware is removed from the dead, the dying, and the horrifically poor when the crew can get away with it. It can handle crude organ-legging and cyberware extraction, not high-end implant surgery or real trauma rescue. Bodies do not linger here as scenery. If someone dies, the body is moved, processed, or removed within a day at most because smell, insects, astral residue, and neighbor attention are bad business. A person walking through knows the room is criminal medicine, but also that the people using it are trying to keep it clean enough to keep working.

Contents:

- Surgical table — old adjustable medical table with patched padding and strap points.
- Rolling instrument trays — washable trays with clamps, scalpels, retractors, cutters, probes, and basic surgical tools.
- Overhead task light — bright articulated lamp over the table.
- Portable suction unit — street-clinic grade unit with sealed waste canister.
- Crash cart shell — wheeled cabinet repurposed for supplies, drugs, and small gear.
- Locking drug cabinet — basic anesthetics, sedatives, painkillers, antibiotics, and restricted medical supplies.
- Sterilizer unit — small countertop sterilizer for instruments.
- Autoclave bags and sterile packs — stacked in labeled bins.
- Biowaste containers — red-labeled sealed bins sized for clinic waste pickup or staged disposal.
- Wash sink — deep medical sink with foot pedal or elbow handles.
- Wall oxygen bottle — portable medical oxygen bottle chained to the wall.
- Medkit station — low-grade stocked medkit and diagnostic slab.
- Tool case for cyberware removal — insulated case with cutters, probes, connectors, and anti-static packing.

## Recovery / Holding Room

The recovery room is smaller and meaner than the medical workroom, but it is still organized. It has two narrow beds, wipe-clean mattresses, privacy curtains that do not quite reach the floor, a portable monitor, a chair, and a locked cabinet. It can hold someone sedated, injured, drugged, or waiting for transport. The lighting is softer than the workroom but still practical, and the air has the same soap-and-plastic smell.

A person walking through understands this is not a place for long care. It can keep a body breathing for a short period, keep someone quiet, or let a badly handled patient stop bleeding enough to move. There are no personal items, no comfort, and no long-term treatment setup. The room exists because moving people immediately is not always possible.

Contents:

- Two narrow recovery beds — wipe-clean mattresses with locking wheels.
- Privacy curtains — cheap medical curtains on ceiling tracks.
- Portable vital monitor — basic monitor for pulse, oxygen, and blood pressure.
- IV stand — wheeled stand with hooks and taped repair points.
- Locked cabinet — dressings, fluids, restraints, gowns, and spare linens.
- Plastic chair — single visitor/staff chair by the wall.
- Restraint straps — stored in cabinet or clipped under the beds.
- Wall clock — cheap battery clock visible from both beds.
- Laundry bin — lidded bin for used sheets, gowns, and towels.

## Cold Storage / Parts Storage

Cold storage is a practical refrigeration room, not a horror display. It is small, sealed, and colder than the rest of the basement, with a commercial cooler, insulated transport coolers, metal shelves, labeled containers, and a drain in the floor. The door seals hard and has a lock separate from the rest of the basement. The room smells like cold plastic, disinfectant, and machine coolant.

The room supports short-term storage only. Organs, tissue, removed cyberware, tools awaiting cleaning, and sealed evidence of work pass through here, but nothing stays longer than the operation can move or sell. Bodies are not stacked here for atmosphere. A corpse that remains on site overnight is a problem to be solved by transport, extraction, disposal, or sale timing, not a permanent feature of the room.

Contents:

- Commercial medical cooler — aging but functional refrigeration unit with internal shelves.
- Insulated transport coolers — hard-sided coolers sized for organs, cyberware, samples, or packed medical goods.
- Metal shelving — sealed containers, cold packs, sterile bags, and labeled supply tubs.
- Locking parts cabinet — anti-static bags, implant cases, tags, and small cyberware packing materials.
- Floor drain — central drain for washdown and condensation.
- Temperature monitor — wall display with cheap alarm buzzer.
- Disposable labels and tags — rolls of stickers, tags, markers, and tape.
- Biohazard bags — sealed bags stored flat in a wall bin.

## Cleaning / Laundry / Decon Corner

This corner is one of the most important parts of the basement because it keeps the rest of the building plausible. It has a washer, dryer, folding surface, mop sink, chemical rack, drying line, laundry bins, rubber gloves, scrub brushes, and sealed bags. It smells aggressively clean, and that smell bleeds upstairs enough to be explained as bar, kitchen, and room cleaning.

A person walking through knows the basement is cleaned often. The setup is not delicate, but it is effective enough for a low-grade operation: wash linens, scrub tools and carts, bag waste, rinse floors, move wet plastic, and reset rooms before smell or stains become obvious. Bleach and soap are cover, but also real working supplies.

Contents:

- Washer and dryer — heavy consumer or light commercial units with worn controls.
- Folding table — plastic surface for linens, gowns, towels, and bagging.
- Mop sink — deep basin with sprayer hose and chemical stains.
- Chemical rack — bleach, detergent, disinfectant, soap, degreaser, and odor control.
- Laundry bins — lidded bins for towels, linens, gowns, and contaminated cloth.
- Rubber gloves and aprons — hanging from wall hooks.
- Scrub brushes and squeegees — wall-mounted cleaning tools.
- Floor fan — used to dry floors after washdown.
- Sealed waste bags — rolls of heavy bags and ties.

## Utility / Mechanical Room

The utility room is cramped and loud, with electrical panels, water heater, breaker boxes, telecom lines, ventilation ducting, and patched service hardware. It gives the basement a normal explanation if someone with shallow authority gets this far: old building, bad utilities, too many repairs, not enough money. The room also explains why the basement has power draw, water use, fans, and service visits.

This room is messy but not random. Cable bundles run toward the production bay, water and drain lines support the clinic and laundry, and the ventilation has been altered enough that a real inspector would ask questions. A person walking through knows the building has been modified past normal residential use, but the modifications are still grounded in utility work, not cinematic hidden architecture.

Contents:

- Electrical panels — labeled and relabeled breaker boxes with taped notes.
- Water heater — oversized or doubled unit supporting laundry, sinks, and cleanup.
- Ventilation ducting — patched ducts and booster fans moving basement air.
- Telecom junction — cheap junction box with hardline feeds and spliced cable runs.
- Tool shelf — pipe tools, wire cutters, tape, fuses, gloves, and spare fittings.
- Spare lock hardware — deadbolts, hasps, hinges, screws, and strike plates.
- Plastic storage tubs — filters, rags, extension cords, and replacement parts.
- Portable work light — caged lamp on a hook or cord reel.

## What a Walkthrough Establishes

A person who walks the basement understands that the operation survives through ordinary service movement, not hidden fantasy infrastructure. Supplies come down the bar stair or through the rear utility exit. Waste, laundry, coolers, cyberware, organs, tools, and bodies leave by the same routes, disguised as maintenance, linen, medical waste, spoiled stock, or contractor cargo. The basement is secure because doors are locked, movement is controlled, and anyone below knows which rooms are allowed to open.

The medical reality is low-grade professional crime. The rooms are clean enough to work, not clean enough to save lives reliably. Cyberware theft, crude organ handling, short recovery, refrigeration, laundry, bleach, soap, and fast removal are the practical facts. Nothing here should feel like a trophy room. The horror is in the ordinary equipment doing exactly what it was set up to do.
