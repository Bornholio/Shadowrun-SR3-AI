# Scumbag - Bitter Lake / Bitter Hills Building Baseline

The chop house is a low-rent residential-service property in **Bitter Lake**, inside the local pocket called **Bitter Hills** by landlords, residents, and street traffic. Bitter Lake is a North Downtown, Low Class, Security C neighborhood, so the building belongs in a place with real city infrastructure but weak practical protection. It is not an antique pre-1990 survivor; it is metroplex-era infill, rebuilt or heavily replaced after older structures stopped being usable. The exterior reads as cheap, ordinary, and current: composite siding panels, sealed windows, patched utility chases, basic sensor-ready wiring, exterior conduit, and repairs done by contractors who cared about passing lease inspection, not making it good.

The building's original purpose was short-lease worker housing with a service-heavy ground/basement core. It served people tied to Aurora traffic, maintenance contracts, nearby light industry, delivery routes, low-end downtown overflow, and civic support work. Two above-ground floors now carry the visible business side: lounge, bar, kitchen, staff room, and four private rooms wired for recording. The basement does not match the casual lease record and is reached through the reinforced supply-closet door behind the bar, preserving the original adventure structure without making the building look purpose-built for crime.

Outside, the property sits on a block that still functions as housing, not a dead vice strip. There are renters, workers, delivery people, tired parents, street-level hustlers, small vendors, and residents who know when to stop looking. The surrounding buildings are mostly practical mid-density Bitter Lake stock: four-to-ten-level apartment slabs, cheap mixed-use blocks, small civic or service buildings, and a few taller towers visible past the block line. The chop house hides because it is not special from the curb. It looks like another badly managed rental that has too much evening traffic and not enough maintenance follow-through.

The building sits on the **north side of an east-west street**, in the **west half of the block**, closer to the industrial/service end than the brighter apartment-and-shop end. Its front faces south toward the street. Its rear faces north into a cluttered service alley, back fences, small garages, trash storage, and utility access. Eastward, the block becomes more public: apartment walkways, corner food, laundry, vending machines, and cheap cameras. Westward, it becomes easier for vans and sealed containers to belong: service yards, delivery bays, repair garages, storage gates, and private security that mostly cares about its own fence.

The approaches should feel different without becoming a menu. From the east, someone comes through light and people: apartment entries, shopfront glow, laundry machines, food steam, parked bikes, bored residents, and cameras that might work. From the west, someone comes through service logic: loading noise, utility fencing, idling vans, shop doors, and drivers who do not ask about every container. From the south, the front approach is visible and socially readable, with the porch and door exposed to the street. From the north, the rear approach is quieter but more intimate, passing fences, back windows, dogs, garage doors, and residents who may remember details later.

The top of the building is a flat service roof, not a dramatic rooftop. It has a shallow equipment curb, cheap weatherproofing, HVAC boxes, vent stacks, patched access panels, utility cable runs, and a small locked roof hatch that maintenance uses when someone complains loudly enough. The roof can be crossed by someone careful, but it is not meant for regular traffic. It is visible from taller apartment walkways to the east and from some rear-facing windows north of the alley. Noise on the roof can matter because the second floor underneath contains the service rooms, editing room, and office.

The roads around it are normal Bitter Lake circulation, not isolated backcountry. Aurora Avenue N / SR-99 is the hard eastern boundary of the neighborhood, with traffic, transit, cheap commerce, and constant movement. Greenwood Avenue N bounds the west side of Bitter Lake, while N 105th and N 145th frame the south and north. Inside those boundaries, the target street is smaller and more local: curb parking, delivery stops, rides dropping people a block away, residents moving between apartments and shops, and enough traffic that one more car is not automatically suspicious.

Entrances are simple and useful. The front door opens into the public-facing lounge/bar flow and is covered by the one cheap camera already established in the adventure. The back door serves staff movement, kitchen trash, smoke breaks, and quiet handling of supplies. The reinforced basement access is inside, behind the bar, disguised as a supply closet door. Windows are sealed or curtained rather than hardened; the building relies on screening, staff, fear, reputation, payoffs, and the three serious crew members more than hardware. That keeps the place believable for Security C Bitter Lake and preserves the adventure's key point: the formal security is weak, but the human operation is dangerous.
